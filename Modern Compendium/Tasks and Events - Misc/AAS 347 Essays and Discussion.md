[Chinese Women, Immigration, and the First U.S. Exclusion Law: The Page Act of 1875](https://www.nps.gov/safr/blogs/chinese-women-immigration-and-the-first-u-s-exclusion-law-the-page-act-of-1875.htm)

The Page Act had three main goals, all focused on preventing immigration from China, Japan, and other parts of “the Orient” (an outdated term for Asian countries). First, the law made it illegal for any U.S. citizen to bring migrants from Asia for the purpose of forced labor. Second, it also restricted the immigration of any convicted criminal who had not completed their sentence. Both provisions were, in practice, almost impossible to enforce.

The third goal of the Page Act was much easier to enforce because it focused on Asian women. While immigration officials might have trouble determining whether an individual was enslaved or a convicted criminal, it was much easier to determine that an individual was a woman.

The Act also gave immigration officials full authority to determine whether an Asian woman was being trafficked "for lewd and immoral purposes."

when we consider the Page Act in the context of extreme anti-Asian rhetoric and violence at the time, we get a broader view of the law’s intent and impact. Although the Act applied to women from all Asian countries, its unspoken focus was on Chinese women.

How are the Page and Cable Acts connected?  How do they limit Asian inclusion into American society?  What were their intended consequences?  How were race, citizenship status, socioeconomic status/class background, and gender legally used in ways to prevent Asians from becoming part of the social, political, cultural and economic fabric of American society?  What are the effects of such laws (series of laws) on Asian American men and women today, in 2026?


The Page and Cable Acts share a connection by which they control the assimilation and development of permanent asian families from developing. The Page Act of 1875 at a glance seems to control human trafficking and contracted laborers but the effects of the act and the wording in code seems to be aimed at oriental immigrants, stated as, "any subject of **China, Japan, or any Oriental country**" and "lewd and immoral purposes." This of course would be in the eyes of US officials that could refuse the permit. At the time it was stereotyped that chinese women were prostitutes so in the end the act would prevent chinese women from immigrating. With the following years came the 1922 Cable Act which was known to control and designate the citizenship of immigrant women and women who were already citizens. To put in short the Cable act deemed a woman's citizenship and their children's citizenship on the eligibility of the husband's citizenship. So if a woman who is a US citizen were to marry that of a oriental immigrant who is ineligible of citizenship, she would lose her citizenship as well as the children that she bears. 

Citizenship status as well as socioeconomic classes were used to distinguish immigrants apart from American society. Due to many Asian immigrants were legally classified as "aliens ineligible for citizenship", American women would face consequences for marrying immigrant men.  To this day the two acts continue to influence Asian Americans today. The laws perpetuated the idea that Asians were these forever foreigners and discouraged the formation of Asian families.
